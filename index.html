The Stayers and the Leavers: Why Some People Stay in One Job Forever and Others Can't

I know a man who has worked at the same bank for thirty-one years. He started as a teller. He is now a branch manager. He drives the same route to work every morning. He eats lunch at the same restaurant every Tuesday and Thursday. He knows everyone's name. Everyone knows his. He will retire from this bank. He will get a watch. He will collect his pension. He is content.

I know a woman who has had seven jobs in ten years. She is not a failure. She is not unreliable. She has been promoted at every stop. But after two years, something shifts. The work becomes routine. The challenges become predictable. She starts looking. She finds something new. She leaves. She will never stay anywhere for thirty-one years. She is content.

Two people. Same economy. Same generation. Same level of competence. Completely different relationships to work.

Why?

The Obvious Answers That Are Wrong

Let me clear some things out of the way first.

It is not laziness. The person who leaves every two years often works harder than the person who stays for thirty. They are constantly proving themselves, learning new systems, building new relationships. That is exhausting. That is not lazy.

It is not lack of discipline. Staying in one job requires discipline. So does leaving. Leaving requires the discipline to update your resume, to network, to learn new skills, to endure rejection. Different discipline. Not less.

It is not fear of commitment. Some leavers are terrified of commitment in relationships but deeply committed to their craft. Some stayers are terrified of change in every domain. The pattern is not consistent.

The real answers are deeper. They are about psychology. About temperament. About what you need from work and what you are willing to tolerate.

What the Stayer Gets

The person who stays in one job for decades is not trapped. Usually. They are making a calculation. Consciously or not, they have decided that the benefits of staying outweigh the benefits of leaving.

What are those benefits?

Predictability. They know what each day will bring. The crises are familiar. The problems are solvable. There is no mystery. For some people, this is peace. For others, it is prison. The stayer finds peace.

Deep relationships. Thirty years in one place means you have watched people's children grow up. You have attended funerals. You have celebrated retirements. You are not just a coworker. You are part of a community. That matters. That is hard to leave.

Accumulated status. The new person has to prove themselves every day. The person who has been there for decades has already proven themselves. They have credibility. They have influence. They have shortcuts that new people do not have. This is valuable.

Tolerance for boredom. This is the quiet one. The stayer can tolerate days that are exactly the same. They do not need novelty to feel alive. They find meaning in mastery, not in variety. They have done the same task ten thousand times and they are very good at it. That is enough.

Risk aversion. Leaving is risky. The new job might be worse. The new boss might be terrible. The new culture might reject you. The stayer has calculated these risks and decided they are not worth taking. The devil they know is better than the devil they do not.

What the Leaver Gets

The person who leaves every few years is also making a calculation. They have decided that the costs of staying outweigh the costs of leaving.

What are they getting?

Novelty. The first six months of a new job are exciting. New problems. New people. New systems to master. The leaver thrives on this. After two years, the novelty wears off. The problems become familiar. The leaver becomes restless. Not because the job is bad. Because the feeling of learning is gone.

Growth. Staying in one job often means growing in depth but not in breadth. You become an expert in one thing. The leaver wants to be competent at many things. They move to learn. They leave because they have mastered the role and there is nothing left to master.

Avoidance of boredom. Boredom is not neutral for the leaver. It is painful. Physically uncomfortable. The leaver would rather struggle with something new than coast through something familiar. The stayer finds comfort in the familiar. The leaver finds suffocation.

Higher tolerance for risk. The leaver is less afraid of the unknown. Or more afraid of the known. Either way, they calculate risk differently. The possibility of a worse job is worth the possibility of a better one. The stayer cannot say that.

A different definition of loyalty. The stayer defines loyalty as staying. The leaver defines loyalty as doing good work while you are there. They are not betraying anyone by leaving. They completed their work. They fulfilled their role. The relationship was transactional. That is not a failure. That is a different value system.

The Personality Research

There is actual research on this. Psychologists have studied what they call "job mobility" and its correlates.

People who change jobs frequently tend to score higher on:

  • Openness to experience (curiosity, creativity, willingness to try new things)
  • Sensation seeking (need for varied, novel experiences)
  • Proactive personality (tendency to take initiative and change circumstances)

People who stay in jobs for long periods tend to score higher on:

  • Conscientiousness (organization, dependability, attention to detail)
  • Agreeableness (cooperation, harmony-seeking)
  • Neuroticism (anxiety, worry about negative outcomes)

These are not value judgments. They are descriptions. High openness and low neuroticism? You are probably a leaver. High conscientiousness and high neuroticism? You are probably a stayer.

Neither is better. Neither is worse. They are just different ways of being in the world.

The Myth of the Entrepreneur

Here is where people get confused.

We assume that people who cannot stay in jobs are destined to start their own businesses. This is sometimes true. Many entrepreneurs have a history of short job tenures. They struggled with authority. They struggled with routine. They needed autonomy.

But many entrepreneurs also have long job tenures before starting their own thing. They learned the industry. They built the network. They saved the capital. Then they left. The leaving was strategic, not temperamental.

And many leavers never start a business. They just move from job to job. They are not founders. They are not failures. They are mercenaries. They sell their skills to whoever needs them. They do not want the responsibility of ownership. They want the freedom to leave when they are bored.

The leaver is not a potential entrepreneur. The leaver is a person who needs novelty and tolerates risk. Those traits can lead to entrepreneurship. They can also lead to a career of interesting jobs at interesting companies. Neither path is wrong.

The Discipline Question

Is it lack of discipline?

No.

The person who stays in one job for thirty years has discipline. The discipline to show up every day. To do work that is no longer exciting. To not quit when frustrated. That is real discipline.

The person who leaves every two years also has discipline. The discipline to learn a new system. To build new relationships. To prove themselves again. To endure the uncertainty of job hunting. That is also real discipline.

They are different skills. They are not the same. But they are both discipline.

The person who cannot stay is not undisciplined. They are mismatched. They are in environments that do not suit their temperament. They need novelty. They are in a job that demands repetition. They need autonomy. They have a boss who micromanages. They need growth. They are in a role that has plateaued.

Leaving is not a failure of discipline. It is a solution to a mismatch.

The Real Problem

The real problem is not the stayer or the leaver. The real problem is that organizations are designed for stayers.

Promotion systems reward tenure. Benefits packages assume long employment. Training programs assume you will be around for years. Mentorship assumes continuity.

The leaver is penalized. Not explicitly. But structurally. They miss out on pensions. They start over at the bottom of the pay scale. They lose seniority. They are seen as flight risks.

The stayer is rewarded. Not because they are better workers. Because the system was designed for them.

This is changing. Slowly. The gig economy. Remote work. Project-based employment. These are making it easier for leavers to thrive. But the old structures remain. Banks. Government. Schools. Hospitals. These still reward staying.

A Final Thought

I do not think people are destined to be one or the other. I think temperament pushes you in a direction. But circumstances pull you in another.

The stayer might have been a leaver if they had been born in a different generation. If they had different financial obligations. If they had a different first job. The leaver might have been a stayer if they had found the right company. The right role. The right boss.

We are not fixed. We are not determined. We make choices within constraints. Some of us choose to stay. Some of us choose to leave. Most of us do both at different points in our lives.

The question is not which one you are. The question is whether you are where you should be. Whether the choice to stay or leave is yours. Whether you are staying because you want to or because you are afraid. Whether you are leaving because you need to or because you are running.

That is the real question. Not discipline. Not destiny. Alignment.

Are you in the right place for who you are right now?

If yes, stay. If no, leave. The rest is noise.

— Guy Fei

The Fine Print You Never Read: What Terms of Use Actually Mean

You have done this a thousand times.

A pop-up appears. A long block of text in a tiny font. A checkbox at the bottom. "I have read and agree to the Terms and Conditions." You click. You do not read. No one reads. There are studies on this. It would take the average person over two hundred hours a year to read every privacy policy and terms of service they agree to. Two hundred hours. That is five full work weeks.

You click. You move on. And somewhere, in that block of text you ignored, you gave away something. Maybe the right to sue. Maybe the right to privacy. Maybe the ownership of your data. Maybe the right to know what the company is doing with your information.

Let me break down what these documents actually mean. Not the legal language. The human language.

The Three Documents

Terms of Use (or Terms of Service)

This is the rulebook. It says what you are allowed to do on the platform and what the platform is allowed to do to you. It covers things like: you cannot post hate speech. You cannot use the service for illegal activity. The company can delete your account without notice if they decide you have violated the rules. You cannot sue them. If there is a dispute, you must use arbitration. You agree to this by using the service.

For the user: you are renting space in someone else's house. They set the rules. They can change the rules whenever they want. They can kick you out whenever they want.

For the company: this document is their shield. It protects them from lawsuits. It limits their liability. It gives them the right to do almost anything they want with your content and your account.

Privacy Policy

This document says what the company does with your data. What information they collect. How they use it. Who they share it with. How long they keep it.

For the user: this is supposed to be transparency. In theory, you read this and decide whether you trust the company with your information. In practice, it is written by lawyers for lawyers. The language is vague. The key details are buried. Most people cannot understand it even if they try.

For the company: this document is their permission slip. By using the service, you are agreeing to their data practices. If you later object, they can point to the privacy policy and say "you agreed."

End User License Agreement (EULA)

This one is less common in social media but very common in software. When you buy software, you do not own it. You are licensing it. The company still owns it. They are just letting you use it under certain conditions.

For the user: you are paying for permission. The software can stop working if the company decides. They can change the terms. They can collect data. You have no ownership.

For the company: this protects their intellectual property. It prevents you from copying, reselling, or reverse-engineering their software.

What They Actually Mean

Here is the truth these documents are hiding behind the legal language.

You have no rights. Not really. You have whatever rights the company chooses to give you. And they can change those rights at any time. Most terms of service include a clause that says "we may update these terms at any time without notice." You agree to future changes by continuing to use the service. You cannot opt out. You cannot negotiate.

Your data is their product. The privacy policy will say they collect data to "improve your experience" or "provide better service." This is not a lie. It is just not the whole truth. They also collect data to sell to advertisers. To train AI. To build profiles that predict your behavior. Your data is valuable. You are giving it away for free.

You cannot sue them. Almost every terms of service includes a binding arbitration clause. This means if there is a dispute, you cannot go to court. You cannot join a class action lawsuit. You must go to a private arbitrator chosen by the company. The odds of winning are low. The process is expensive. Most people do not bother.

They own your content. Post a photo on Instagram? You still own the photo. But you have given them a license to use it anywhere, anytime, for any purpose. They can put your face in an ad. They can sell your photo to a stock image site. They can use your words to train an AI that replaces you. You agreed to this.

They can delete you without explanation. Your account can disappear. Your posts, your photos, your messages, your connections—all gone. No warning. No appeal. No explanation. The terms of service allow this. You agreed.

What Happens When They Are Violated

There are two kinds of violations.

You violate the terms. You post something prohibited. You use the service for something illegal. You try to game the system. The company detects this. They may warn you. They may suspend you. They may delete your account. You have no recourse. The decision is final. You agreed to this.

The company violates the terms. This is rarer. Because the terms are written by the company. They are designed to make it very difficult for the company to violate them. The terms give the company broad discretion. Almost everything is permitted. But sometimes, a company goes too far. Sometimes, they are caught.

Examples

Facebook and Cambridge Analytica (2018)

Facebook's privacy policy said they would not share user data without permission. But they allowed a researcher to access the data of eighty-seven million users. The researcher sold that data to Cambridge Analytica, a political consulting firm. The firm used the data to target political ads. Users did not know. They had not agreed.

What happened: Facebook was fined five billion dollars by the US Federal Trade Commission. The largest fine ever imposed on a technology company. But the damage was done. The data had already been used. The election had already happened. Users received a notice in their feed. Most scrolled past it.

Google and location tracking (2018)

Google's privacy policy said users could turn off location tracking. But an investigation by the Associated Press found that Google continued to track location even when users turned the setting off. Google was storing location data from other sources—Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cell towers—even when the user had explicitly disabled location services.

What happened: Google settled with several US states for over three hundred million dollars. They changed their privacy policy to be more transparent. They continued collecting data. Most users never knew.

Uber and the data breach cover-up (2016)

Hackers stole the data of fifty-seven million Uber users and drivers. Names. Email addresses. Phone numbers. Driver's license numbers. Uber did not disclose the breach. Instead, they paid the hackers one hundred thousand dollars to delete the data and keep quiet. They hid the breach for over a year.

What happened: The new CEO discovered the cover-up. Several executives were fired. Uber paid one hundred and forty-eight million dollars in settlements. Criminal charges were filed against the chief security officer. Users were notified more than a year after the breach. By then, the data was already in the wild.

TikTok and child privacy (2022)

TikTok's privacy policy claimed they protected the data of young users. But investigations found that TikTok was collecting personal information from children without parental consent. Email addresses. Phone numbers. Device locations. All from users under thirteen.

What happened: TikTok paid ninety-two million dollars to settle a class action lawsuit. They changed their data practices for children. They continued collecting data from adults.

Do Users Know When Terms Are Violated

Almost never.

Companies are not required to tell you when they violate their own policies. They are required to tell you when there is a data breach. But a data breach is a specific thing—unauthorized access by an external hacker. When the company itself violates the terms, that is not a breach. That is just... business.

You might learn about it years later, if the violation is discovered by journalists or regulators. You might see a news headline. You might receive a notice in the mail that you throw away because it looks like junk. You might be part of a class action settlement that gives you three dollars and ninety-seven cents.

But at the moment of violation—when your data is being sold, when your location is being tracked, when your content is being used without your knowledge—you do not know. You cannot know. The system is designed to keep you in the dark.

How to Protect Yourself

There is no perfect solution. The system is rigged. But there are small steps that make a difference.

Read the parts that matter. You will not read the whole document. No one will. But you can search for key phrases: "arbitration," "data sharing," "third parties," "license to use," "without notice." If these phrases appear, you know what you are agreeing to.

Use privacy-focused alternatives. Not every company sells your data. Signal instead of WhatsApp. DuckDuckGo instead of Google. Firefox instead of Chrome. These are not perfect. They are better.

Opt out where you can. Many privacy policies include an opt-out for data sharing. The option is buried. The language is confusing. But it exists. Look for "Do Not Sell My Personal Information" or "Privacy Settings."

Use a burner email. Do not use your real email for every service. Create a separate email for accounts you do not trust. Use temporary email services for one-time signups.

Check permissions regularly. Your phone knows where you are. Your microphone listens. Your camera watches. Go to your settings. Review which apps have which permissions. Turn off anything that does not need to be on.

Assume nothing is private. This is the bleak one. Assume that anything you put online will eventually be public. Assume that your data is being collected. Assume that your messages are being read. Act accordingly.

Support regulation. The real solution is not individual action. It is collective action. Laws like the GDPR in Europe give users rights. Companies must ask for permission. They must explain what they are doing. They must delete your data if you ask. Tanzania does not have these laws yet. It should.

The Bottom Line

The terms of use are not for you. They are for the company. They protect the company. They limit your rights. They give the company permission to do almost anything.

You click "agree" because you have no choice. The service will not work otherwise. The company knows this. They are counting on it.

The real protection is not reading the fine print. The real protection is understanding that the fine print is a trap. And acting like it. Be careful what you share. Assume nothing is safe. And when you can, choose services that respect you—not because their terms say so, but because their business model depends on it.

Those services are rare. They exist. Find them. Support them. And until then, protect yourself like no one else will.

Because no one else will.

The Price of Trying: What It Really Costs to Start a Business in Tanzania

Let me tell you about two people.

One had an idea. A small restaurant near the bus stand. She had saved for two years. She knew the location, knew the customers, knew the menu. She rented the space. She bought the chairs. She painted the walls. And then she waited. For the license. For the inspection. For the person who had the stamp. She waited six months. The rent did not wait. The chairs gathered dust. By the time the license came, the savings were gone. The restaurant opened. It closed three months later.

The other had an idea too. A phone repair shop. He knew someone who knew someone. The license took two weeks. The inspection happened the next day. The person with the stamp was his cousin's husband. He opened on time. He made profit in the first month. He expanded the next year.

Same city. Same economy. Same dream. Different outcomes.

The difference was not hard work. Not the business plan. Not the quality of the idea. The difference was access. The difference was who you know. The difference was the real cost of starting—the cost that doesn't appear on any government website, the cost you cannot budget for, the cost that decides who succeeds and who fails before they even begin.

The Official Story

The Tanzania Investment Centre will tell you that starting a business is straightforward. Register your company. Obtain your taxpayer identification number. Apply for your license. Open a bank account. Five steps. Two weeks. A few hundred thousand shillings.

This is not a lie. It is just incomplete.

The official fees are real. The registration fee. The license fee. The stamp duty. The notary. The photocopies. The passport photos. The file folder they require that you must buy from the vendor outside because the one you brought is the wrong color.

These costs add up. For a small business, ten thousand here, twenty thousand there, fifty thousand for something you didn't expect. By the time you have the papers, you have spent money you planned to spend on stock. On rent. On the sign above your door.

But the official fees are not the problem. They are the beginning.

The Unofficial Costs

The person who processes your application has a desk. On that desk, there is a pile. Your application is in the pile. The pile does not move unless something happens. The something can be time. Weeks. Months. The something can be a phone call from someone important. The something can be an envelope.

You are not told to pay a bribe. No one says the word. The person says: "This will take time. Unless..." And the sentence trails off. You are supposed to complete it yourself.

Some people complete it. They pay. The application moves. The license comes. The business opens.

Some people do not. They wait. The pile does not move. The weeks become months. The rent accumulates. The stock they already bought sits in boxes. The dream they saved for begins to sour.

This is not corruption in the dramatic sense. No bags of money change hands. No one goes to prison. It is small. It is quiet. It is the expectation that things will not happen unless you make them happen. And making them happen costs.

The Waiting

The waiting is its own cost.

You cannot open without the license. You cannot operate without the inspection. You cannot serve customers without the certificate. So you wait. And while you wait, you pay rent. You pay for the space you cannot use. You pay for the equipment that sits idle. You pay for the staff you hired who have nothing to do.

Time is money. Everyone knows this. But no one calculates the cost of waiting into their business plan. No one budgets for the three months when you pay and pay and pay and earn nothing.

The person with connections does not wait. The person with the right phone number makes a call. The pile shifts. The license appears. The waiting is for other people.

The Network

Here is the hardest truth.

The most important factor in whether your business succeeds is not your idea. It is not your capital. It is not your hard work. It is your network. The people you know. The people who know you. The people who will pick up the phone when you call.

The person with the cousin in the licensing office does not need to bribe anyone. The cousin makes a call. The application moves. No envelope changes hands. Nothing illegal happened. The system worked. It just worked faster for that person than for the person who did not have a cousin.

The person who went to the right school, who played football with the right people, who attended the right weddings—that person has a network. That person can start a business. That person can solve problems that would sink someone else.

This is not a secret. Everyone knows it. Everyone talks about it. But no one writes it down. Because writing it down feels like giving up. Like saying the system is broken and cannot be fixed.

But the system is broken. And the first step to fixing it is naming it.

Who Starts Anyway

Given all of this, why does anyone start a business?

Some start because they have no choice. No job. No salary. No regular income. The business is not a dream. It is survival. They sell vegetables. They repair phones. They braid hair. They do not register. They do not get a license. They operate in the gaps, invisible, small, untaxed. They are not entrepreneurs. They are people trying to eat.

Some start because they have the network. The cousin. The friend. The connection. They know the shortcuts. They know who to call. For them, the system works. It is slow, yes. It is frustrating, yes. But it works.

Some start because they are stubborn. They know the costs. They know the waiting. They know they might fail. They start anyway. They register. They wait. They pay. They call. They follow up. They go back. They stand in lines. They bring the passport photos again because the first set expired. They do not give up. Most of them fail. Some of them succeed. The ones who succeed are not smarter. They are not harder working. They are luckier. Or they had just enough money to outlast the waiting.

What We Lose

Every person who does not start a business is a loss. Not just to them. To everyone.

The restaurant that never opened. The tailoring shop that stayed a dream. The delivery service that existed only in someone's head. These are jobs that were never created. Services that were never offered. Competition that never arrived to drive down prices.

The system that makes starting hard does not hurt the person who fails. It hurts everyone who would have eaten at that restaurant. Everyone who would have bought from that tailor. Everyone who would have used that delivery service.

We do not count these losses. They are invisible. The business that never exists leaves no trace. But the loss is real. It is the cost of a system that rewards connections over ideas, patience over efficiency, luck over planning.

A Question Without An Answer

I do not know how to fix this. Not easily. Not quickly.

The people who benefit from the current system are the people who could change it. They have no incentive to change it. The system works for them. Their cousins get licenses. Their friends get inspections. Their businesses open on time.

The people who are hurt by the system are the people who cannot change it. They have no power. They have no voice. They have no cousin.

So the system continues. And every year, people save money. People dream. People plan. People rent spaces they cannot use. People wait for licenses that do not come. People give up. People go back to jobs they hate because starting a business was too expensive in ways no one told them about.

That is the real cost. Not the fees. Not the bribes. The dreams that die before they have a chance to live.

The Cat, The Multiverse, and The Choice You Didn't Make

Part Two of a Series on Things Beyond Understanding

Let me tell you three stories. They are the same story.

The Cat

A cat is placed in a steel box with a radioactive atom, a Geiger counter, and a vial of poison. The atom has a fifty percent chance of decaying within one hour. If it decays, the cat dies. If it doesn't, the cat lives.

Quantum mechanics says: until you open the box, the atom exists in both states. Decayed and not decayed. The cat, therefore, is both dead and alive. The two possibilities coexist. The universe contains both outcomes simultaneously.

This is not a metaphor. This is the math. The wave function that describes the atom includes both possibilities. The equations do not collapse into one outcome until measurement occurs. Until you open the box, reality is undecided.

Schrödinger invented this thought experiment to mock the interpretation. He meant to show how absurd it was to apply quantum rules to everyday objects. But the joke failed. The cat became famous. And physicists spent the next century arguing about what it actually means.

Here is what everyone agrees on: before observation, the atom is in superposition. Both possibilities are real, mathematically. After observation, we see one outcome. The other disappears.

What happens in between? No one knows.

Some say the wave function collapses. Reality chooses. The other possibility was never real—it was just a calculation.

Some say the universe splits. Both outcomes continue. The cat lives in one universe and dies in another. You just happen to be in the universe where you see what you see.

Some say consciousness causes the collapse. The act of a mind observing reality forces the choice. Without a mind, the cat remains suspended.

The experiments cannot tell the difference. The math works either way. The mystery remains.

The Multiverse

If the universe splits at every quantum event, the number of universes is not just large. It is infinite. Multiplying every second. Every radioactive decay. Every photon hitting a screen. Every decision a particle makes.

In one universe, the cat lives. In another, it dies. In one universe, you turn left. In another, you turn right. In one universe, you apply for the job. In another, you don't. In one universe, you tell them you love them. In another, you stay silent.

All of these universes are real. All of them continue. You just don't see them.

The other you is not you. Not anymore. You diverged at the moment of the split. You share a history up to that point. After that, you are different people. Living different lives. Making different choices. Experiencing different outcomes.

You cannot visit the other branches. You cannot communicate with the other you. The branches do not interact. They are separate. Real, but separate.

This is the Many-Worlds Interpretation. It takes the math literally. It says: the wave function never collapses. It just branches. Every possibility happens. Nothing is lost. Nothing is chosen. Everything simply... continues.

It is a beautiful idea. It is also untestable. You cannot observe the other branches. You cannot prove they exist. You can only follow the math to its logical conclusion and decide whether you believe it.

Some physicists do. Most don't. But no one has disproven it.

The Time Travel Trade-Off

Now add time travel.

Imagine you go back to yesterday. You change something. You stop yourself from saying the wrong thing. You buy the lottery ticket you meant to buy. You turn left instead of right.

What happens to the timeline you left behind?

One possibility: it disappears. You have overwritten your own history. The original timeline never existed. You remember it, but it is gone. This creates paradoxes. If you changed the past, why do you remember the original? How did you know to go back?

Another possibility: the timeline does not disappear. It continues. You have simply created a new branch. The original you continues in the original universe, unaware that another version of you traveled back and started a new timeline. The two branches coexist. They just don't interact.

This is the trade-off. You can change the past. But you cannot return to your original future. You are now in a new branch. The original branch continues without you. The change comes with a cost. You lose what you left behind.

This is sometimes called the many-worlds interpretation of time travel. Each trip to the past creates a new universe. The original timeline is preserved. The traveler simply cannot go back to it.

The trade-off is not equal. You gain a different outcome. You lose your original world. Whether that is worth it depends on what you changed and what you left behind.

The Human Part

Now let me tell you the same story again. Without the physics.

You apply for a job. You wait. In the silence of the waiting, both outcomes exist. You could get the job. You could not. The universe contains both possibilities. You live in superposition. Your friend asks, "Did you get it?" You say, "I don't know." You are the cat in the box. Undecided. Both alive and dead.

Then the phone rings. You got it. The wave function collapses. The other possibility disappears. You never know what would have happened if you hadn't gotten it. That universe is gone. Closed. Inaccessible.

But was it ever real? Did it exist before the phone rang? Or was it just a possibility, a calculation, a hope?

The physicists don't know. Neither do you.

—

You tell someone you love them. You wait for an answer. The silence contains both yes and no. Both are possible. Both feel real. You are suspended. The cat is in the box. The atom has not yet decayed.

Then they speak. They say yes. The wave function collapses. The no disappears. You never know what would have happened if they had said no. That universe is gone.

But somewhere, in some branch of the multiverse, they said no. Some version of you is living that outcome. Some version of you is heartbroken. Some version of you is learning to move on. That version is real. You just don't see it.

You cannot visit that branch. You cannot know what happened there. You can only live in the branch you are in.

—

You think about going back. Changing something. The thing you said. The thing you didn't say. The choice you made. The choice you avoided.

If you could go back, would you? And if you did, what would you lose?

The trade-off is this: you can change the past. But you cannot return to the future you left behind. The original timeline continues without you. The people you knew continue without you. The life you lived continues without you.

You gain a different outcome. You lose your original world.

Is that worth it? For a job? For a love? For a single word spoken differently?

Most people say no. Most people stay in their branch. They live with the outcomes they have. They mourn the possibilities that disappeared. They wonder about the universes they cannot see.

But they stay.

The Connection

Here is what ties these together.

The cat. The multiverse. The time travel trade-off. All of them are about the same thing: the relationship between possibility and reality.

Before measurement, both possibilities exist. After measurement, only one remains. The other disappears. Or it continues, invisibly, in a branch you cannot access. Or it remains accessible only if you are willing to lose everything you have.

The physics is uncertain. The interpretations are disputed. The experiments are inconclusive.

But the human experience is clear.

You live in a world of closed doors. Every choice you make closes other doors. Every path you take abandons other paths. Every yes is also a thousand nos.

The cat dies or lives. You never see both. The multiverse says both happen, but you only see one. Time travel says you could go back and choose differently, but you would lose what you had.

The physics is about atoms and wave functions. The human part is about jobs and love and the silence before an answer.

They are the same story.

I cannot tell you whether the multiverse is real. I cannot tell you whether time travel is possible. I cannot tell you what happens to the possibilities you did not choose.

No one can. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

But I can tell you this: the feeling of standing at a crossroads, of waiting for an answer, of wondering what would have happened if you had turned left instead of right—that feeling is not just psychology. It is physics. It is the shape of reality at its deepest level.

The cat is in the box. The atom has not yet decayed. You are waiting for the phone to ring.

Both outcomes are still possible.

Both are still real.

And then the phone rings. And one of them disappears forever.

That is the mystery. That is the science. That is the human experience.

All the same story.

The Ghost in the Present: Understanding Déjà Vu

Part One of a Series on Things Beyond Understanding

You are sitting somewhere. A café, maybe. Or a bus. Or a room you've never entered before.

Someone speaks. You recognize the words before they finish the sentence. The light falls across the table in a way that feels familiar. The temperature of the air. The sound of traffic outside. All of it has happened before. Not similar. Not reminiscent. Exactly this. The same conversation. The same angle of the sun. The same pause before the next word.

You know this moment. You have lived it already.

Then, as quickly as it came, the feeling passes. You shake your head. You laugh at yourself. Déjà vu. Just a brain glitch. Nothing real.

But for those few seconds, you were certain. Certain that time had folded. Certain that you had seen the future or remembered a past life or stepped into a parallel version of yourself. Certain of something that cannot possibly be true.

And then you weren't.

What happened?

The Shape of the Mystery

Let me start by saying what déjà vu is not.

It is not a prediction coming true. It is not proof of past lives. It is not a glitch in the matrix. These are fun explanations. They are not necessary. The phenomenon is strange enough without them.

Déjà vu is the strong, unsettling feeling that a current experience has been experienced before. The key word is "feeling." You are not actually remembering. You are feeling like you are remembering. The brain has generated a sensation of familiarity without any actual memory to attach it to.

This happens to about two-thirds of people. Some experience it frequently. Some, rarely. A small number never experience it at all. It peaks in young adulthood and declines with age. It is more common in people who are tired, stressed, or sleep-deprived. It lasts seconds.

And no one knows exactly why.

What Science Has Figured Out

There are theories. Good ones. But no consensus.

The leading theory involves a delay in neural processing. Here's how it works: your brain processes information through multiple pathways. Normally, they arrive at the same time. But sometimes, one pathway lags. The information arrives late—milliseconds late, not seconds.

By the time the late information arrives, your brain has already processed the moment once. So when the second stream of information comes in, your brain tags it as familiar. Not because it's a memory. Because the brain has literally seen this information before, a split second ago, and now it's seeing it again.

The result: you experience the present moment twice, almost simultaneously. Once in real time. Once in a neural echo. And your brain, trying to make sense of this, generates the feeling: "I've been here before."

That's the theory. It's elegant. It explains why déjà vu is often accompanied by a sense of knowing what will happen next—because your brain has already processed the next millisecond and is feeding you the feeling that you've seen it.

But it's not proven. It's hard to test. You can't reliably trigger déjà vu in a lab.

The Brain Regions Involved

What we do know is which parts of the brain are involved.

The medial temporal lobe, particularly the hippocampus and surrounding areas, is critical for memory and familiarity. Damage to these areas can cause a loss of déjà vu. People with certain forms of epilepsy experience déjà vu as a seizure symptom—the brain's memory circuits firing abnormally.

This is important. It tells us that déjà vu is not a psychological quirk. It's neurological. It has a physical basis. Something happens in the brain's memory systems, and the feeling of false familiarity is the result.

The frontal lobes, involved in reality monitoring, usually catch the error. "Wait," they say, "this can't be real. You've never been here before." That's why the feeling lasts only seconds. Your brain corrects itself.

In people with certain conditions, the correction doesn't happen. The feeling persists. That's when déjà vu becomes something else—a delusion, a seizure, a break from reality. For most of us, the system works. The ghost appears, then disappears.

The Variants

Déjà vu has cousins.

Déjà vécu: "already lived." Stronger than déjà vu. The feeling that you have not just seen this moment but lived through it in detail. You know what happens next. You feel like you could predict the next few seconds. This is rarer. More disturbing.

Déjà entendu: "already heard." The feeling that you have heard a conversation or a piece of music before, even though you know you haven't.

Déjà visité: "already visited." The feeling that you have been to a place before, even though you are certain you haven't. You know what's around the corner. You recognize the smell of the air.

And the opposite: jamais vu. "never seen." The feeling of unfamiliarity with something that should be familiar. You look at a word you've written a thousand times. It looks strange. Wrong. Like you've never seen it before. You look at your own face in the mirror. It doesn't feel like you.

Jamais vu is rarer. It's also less studied. But it suggests that the same brain systems that generate false familiarity can also suppress real familiarity. The memory circuits can fail in both directions.

What Déjà Vu Is Not Telling Us

Here's where I want to be careful.

Déjà vu feels profound. It feels like a window into something deeper—past lives, parallel universes, the simulation hypothesis. The feeling is so strong, so certain, that it's hard to believe it's just a brain glitch.

But the evidence suggests it is just a brain glitch. A fascinating one. A mysterious one. But not a supernatural one.

The people who experience déjà vu most frequently are people with temporal lobe epilepsy. Their brains generate the feeling as a side effect of abnormal electrical activity. If the feeling can be produced by a seizure, it's probably not a message from another dimension.

That doesn't make it less interesting. It makes it more interesting. The brain generates reality. Sometimes the generation goes wrong. And we get to watch the machinery hiccup.

The Question That Remains

Even if we accept the neural delay theory, even if we map the brain regions, even if we categorize the variants—the fundamental mystery remains.

Why does it feel like that?

Why does a processing error produce a feeling of certainty? Why does a millisecond delay generate the experience of having lived an entire moment before? Why isn't the brain just confused? Why does confusion feel like conviction?

The answer, I think, is that the brain is not designed to know when it's wrong. It is designed to produce the most plausible explanation for whatever is happening. When the memory system signals "familiar," the brain accepts that signal as real. It doesn't check. It doesn't doubt. It just reports: this has happened before.

The doubt comes later. The correction comes later. The feeling comes first. And the feeling is what we call déjà vu.

A Final Thought

I don't have an answer. No one does.

That's why this belongs in a series about things beyond understanding. We know more about déjà vu than we did twenty years ago. We know which brain regions light up. We have theories about processing delays. We can induce it in people with electrical stimulation.

But no one can tell you why it happens to you, at that specific moment, in that specific café. No one can explain why the feeling is so strong and so brief. No one can say, definitively, what the brain is doing when it tricks itself into remembering a past that never occurred.

The mystery remains. Small. Brief. Inconsequential. But real.

And sometimes, the small mysteries are the best ones. Because they remind us that even the most ordinary experience—sitting in a chair, drinking coffee, hearing a friend speak—is not fully explained. Not really. Not yet.

The Lost Art of Useless Play

Watch a child alone with a stick.

The stick is not a stick. It is a sword. It is a wand. It is a bridge between two worlds that only exists in the child's head. The child talks to the stick. The stick answers. There are rules to this game, but the child made them up and can change them at any time. No one is watching. No one is scoring. No one will give a trophy or a grade.

The child plays for forty-five minutes. Then drops the stick and runs inside for juice. The stick returns to being a stick. Nothing was produced. Nothing was achieved. Nothing was learned that can be tested.

And yet, something happened. Something that adults, for the most part, have lost.

What Play Actually Is

Let's define terms.

Play is not a game. Games have rules, winners, losers, objectives. Play is not a sport. Sports have training, performance, evaluation. Play is not a hobby. Hobbies produce something—a chair, a painting, a garden.

Play is activity with no purpose beyond itself. It is imagining. It is pretending. It is doing something because the doing feels good, not because the outcome matters.

The stick becomes a sword. The blanket becomes a cape. The cardboard box becomes a spaceship. And for the duration of the play, these things are true. Not metaphorically true. Really true. The child is not pretending the box is a spaceship. The child is in the spaceship.

Adults cannot do this. Or can, but feel ridiculous trying.

What Happens in the Brain

There is research on this, though not as much as there should be. Because researchers, like the rest of us, have trouble taking play seriously.

What we know: play activates the brain's default mode network. This is the network that comes online when you are not focused on a task. When you are daydreaming. When you are letting your mind wander. It is the creative network. The associative network. The network that makes connections between things that don't obviously belong together.

In children, this network is highly active during play. The child is not just having fun. The child is building the architecture of creative thinking. Learning how to generate possibilities. Practicing the skill of "what if."

In adults, the default mode network is often suppressed. We spend our days in task-positive networks. Focused. Goal-directed. Efficient. The brain learns that useful thinking is directed thinking. Wandering is wasted time.

Play also activates the brain's reward systems, but differently from winning. Winning releases dopamine at the moment of victory. Play releases dopamine continuously, in small pulses, as the activity unfolds. The pleasure is in the process, not the outcome.

Adults chase the big dopamine hit. Children understand the small ones.

When We Stop

No one decides to stop playing. It just... fades.

Sometime in late childhood, play becomes embarrassing. You are too old for imaginary friends. Too old for pretending. The other kids will laugh. The adults will look at you strangely. The world starts asking: what are you making? What are you learning? What is the point?

School accelerates this. School is about outcomes. Grades. Measurements. Right answers and wrong answers. There is no grade for imagination. There is no test for "what if." The child who spends an hour playing with a stick is the child who didn't spend an hour studying.

Work finishes the job. Work is the opposite of play. Work has deliverables. Work has metrics. Work has a boss who asks "what did you accomplish today?" You cannot answer "I turned a stick into a sword." Not if you want to keep your job.

By adulthood, play has been trained out of most of us. Not deliberately. Just gradually. The neural pathways that supported play weaken from disuse. The muscles of imagination atrophy.

What remains is consumption. Watching other people play sports. Watching other people play games. Watching other people pretend. But the active doing—the stick in your own hand—feels foreign.

What We Lose

Here is what the research suggests we lose when we stop playing.

First, creative problem-solving. Play is the original design thinking. You try things without fear of failure because there is no failure. The stick works as a sword or it doesn't. Either way, you learn something. Adults who play are better at generating novel solutions because they have kept the muscle that generates possibilities without judging them.

Second, stress regulation. Play activates the parasympathetic nervous system. The rest-and-digest system. The opposite of fight-or-flight. Children move in and out of stress states fluidly because they play. Adults get stuck in high alert because they don't.

Third, social bonding. Watch children play together. They negotiate rules constantly. "No, you can't do that." "Okay, but then I get to do this." They are learning the grammar of cooperation. Adult play—real play, not competitive games—does the same thing. But adults rarely allow themselves to be that vulnerable with each other.

Fourth, joy. Not happiness. Happiness is the satisfaction of a goal achieved. Joy is the feeling of being fully present in an activity that has no goal. Children have joy. Adults have happiness. And happiness, research shows, is less durable.

The Exception

Some adults keep the ability to play.

They are not necessarily creative professionals. Some are. But many are ordinary people in ordinary jobs who have simply never lost the capacity to be ridiculous in private.

They talk to themselves. They make up songs about mundane tasks. They narrate their dog's inner monologue. They dance in the kitchen when no one is watching.

What distinguishes them? Often, a lack of self-consciousness. Or a conscious rejection of it. They have decided that the judgment of imaginary observers is not worth sacrificing the pleasure of play. They have protected a small territory of uselessness in a world that demands utility.

Research on aging suggests that people who maintain playfulness into old age have better cognitive outcomes. Not because play is brain training. Because play keeps the brain flexible. Keeps it from hardening into routine. Keeps the default mode network online.

The people who stop playing don't just lose fun. They lose a kind of mental plasticity. They become rigid. Predictable. The kind of adult who cannot imagine a different way because they have not practiced imagining.

Can You Get It Back

I think so. But it takes deliberate effort.

The first step is permission. You have to give yourself permission to be useless. To do something that produces nothing. To look ridiculous. To fail at something that doesn't matter because nothing matters in play.

The second step is starting small. Not "I will now play for an hour." That is too much pressure. That is performance. That is work. Start with thirty seconds. Make a sound for no reason. Move your body in a way that has no purpose. Narrate what you are doing in a silly voice.

The third step is finding a container. Play is easier when it has boundaries. A time limit. A private space. A single object. Children play infinitely because they have no other demands. Adults need to carve out the space.

The fourth step is letting go of the audience. The hardest part. The voice that says "you look stupid" is not your voice. It is the voice of everyone who ever told you to grow up. You can ignore it. It takes practice. But you can ignore it.

The Stick

I keep coming back to the stick.

A child sees a stick and sees a hundred possibilities. An adult sees a stick and sees a stick. The adult is not wrong. The stick is objectively a stick. But the child is not wrong either. The child sees what the stick could be.

The loss of play is the loss of that "could be." The loss of possibility. The loss of imagination as a living, daily practice.

You cannot run a business on "could be." You cannot pay rent on "could be." You cannot raise children on "could be." The adult world requires the actual. The real. The measurable.

But you can keep a small corner. A closet. A drawer. A Saturday morning. A stick.

Not because it will make you more productive. Not because it will help you live longer. Not because there is any practical reason at all.

Because the ability to see what a stick could be is the same ability that lets you see what your life could be. And that ability, once lost, is very hard to find again.

The Psychology of People Who Can't Be Wrong

Part One of a Series

You've met this person.

Maybe you work with them. Maybe you're related to them. Maybe, if you're honest with yourself, you see fragments of them in your own reflection.

They are not necessarily arrogant. Some of them are quiet. Some of them are even shy. But watch closely when someone corrects them. Watch what happens when evidence contradicts their position. Watch their face when they realize, deep down, that they might have made a mistake.

They cannot say: "I didn't know that."

They cannot say: "You're right."

They cannot say: "I was wrong."

Instead, they pivot. They justify. They change the subject. They get angry. They get quiet. They rewrite history so that they were never really wrong to begin with—just misunderstood, or missing context, or working with different information.

This is not stubbornness. Not really. Stubbornness is a choice. This is something deeper. Something automatic. Something protective.

What's Actually Happening

For most people, being wrong is uncomfortable. For the person who can't be wrong, being wrong feels like annihilation.

The psychologist Carol Dweck, who studies mindset, would say these people have a fixed view of their own intelligence and worth. They believe that ability is static. You either have it or you don't. And if you're wrong about something, that means you don't have it. That means you are not smart. Not capable. Not valuable.

So being wrong isn't a small correction. It's an existential threat.

There's research backing this. Studies on cognitive dissonance show that when people hold a belief and encounter evidence that contradicts it, their brains react the same way they would to a physical threat. The amygdala lights up. Stress hormones release. The body prepares to defend itself—not against a predator, but against an idea.

For most people, that threat response passes. They feel the discomfort, adjust their belief, and move on.

For the person who can't be wrong, the threat response doesn't pass. It escalates. Because the belief being threatened isn't just "the capital of France is Lyon." It's "I am a person who knows things. I am a person who is competent. I am a person who deserves respect."

Admit you were wrong about the capital of France? That's nothing. Admit you were wrong about something that matters to your identity? That feels like dying a little.

The Defense Mechanisms

Watch what they do when cornered.

Some deflect. "That's not what I meant." They reframe the original statement so it was never really incorrect—just poorly expressed. The facts didn't change. The interpretation did.

Some attack. "Why are you being so aggressive?" They shift the focus from the content of the argument to your tone, your timing, your character. Now the conversation isn't about whether they were wrong. It's about whether you're being fair.

Some disappear. They go quiet. They change the subject. They leave the room. Not because they're storming off in anger, but because staying means admitting something they cannot afford to admit.

Some rewrite. Hours or days later, they will return with a new version of events. "I never said that." "You must have misunderstood." "What I actually meant was..." The past bends to protect the present self.

None of this is calculated manipulation. Most of the time, they believe their own revisions. The brain is remarkably good at protecting the ego by editing memory.

Where This Comes From

People don't arrive at this trait by accident.

Often, it's learned in childhood. A home where mistakes were punished harshly. A parent whose love felt conditional on performance. A teacher who humiliated rather than corrected. A culture that equated being wrong with being weak.

If you grow up learning that errors are unacceptable, you develop strategies to avoid ever admitting them. Not because you're dishonest. Because you're scared.

Sometimes, it's learned in adulthood. A high-stakes profession where confidence matters more than accuracy. A relationship where being wrong meant losing ground. A social circle that rewarded certainty and punished doubt.

Sometimes, it's just habit. Years of getting away with it. Years of people backing down because arguing with you is exhausting. The behavior works, so it continues.

But beneath all of these is the same current: a self so fragile that it cannot survive the small death of being wrong.

The Cost

Here's the tragedy.

The person who can't be wrong doesn't protect their self-worth. They trap it.

Because admitting you're wrong is how you learn. It's how you grow. It's how you get better at things. The person who can't say "I didn't know that" also can't say "teach me." The person who can't say "you're right" also can't say "I see what you mean."

They stay where they are. Not because they lack the capacity to improve, but because improvement requires first admitting that where you are is not the finish line.

And the people around them? They learn to stop correcting. They learn to stop arguing. They learn to stop sharing information that might trigger a defensive spiral. The relationship becomes a minefield, and the easiest path is to say nothing at all.

The person who can't be wrong ends up surrounded by silence. Not because people agree with them. Because people have given up.

A Small Note on Self-Examination

I said at the beginning that you might see fragments of this in yourself.

Most of us do.

There are things we're defensive about. Areas where being wrong feels too expensive. Topics tied to our identity, our training, our sense of who we are.

The difference between "someone who can't be wrong" and "someone who struggles to admit error in specific domains" is flexibility. Can you admit you were wrong about something small today? Can you laugh at yourself? Can you thank someone for correcting you, even if it stings?

If yes, you're not the person in this article. You're just human.

If no—if the thought of admitting error makes your chest tight and your thoughts spiral—then maybe it's worth asking why. Not to shame yourself. To understand.

Because the cage is not being wrong. The cage is being unable to say so.

The Question This Leaves

I don't have a tidy solution here. People who can't be wrong rarely read articles about themselves and think "aha, that's me." The defensiveness prevents the recognition.

But for the people who deal with them—the coworkers, the family members, the friends—there is a useful question.

Do you need them to admit they're wrong? Or do you just need them to change the behavior?

Because those are different goals. One requires a confrontation. The other might be achievable through small, repeated redirections. Not "you're wrong about that." But "here's what I see, and here's what I'm going to do based on what I see."

You cannot force someone to see themselves clearly. You can only decide how much energy you're willing to spend trying.

The Words We Wear Thin

There's a set of words we use so often they've lost their edges. Resilience. Obsession. Commitment. Passion. Patience.

We toss them into job applications and Instagram captions. We claim them like participation medals. And somewhere along the way, we forgot that these words originally described something much harder, much stranger, much more specific than we now admit.

Let's walk through what they actually mean. Not the dictionary versions. The real ones.

Resilience

What people think it means: Bouncing back. Being tough. Not crying when things go wrong.

What it actually means: The ability to be broken and still function. Not returning to who you were before the damage, but building something new that incorporates the crack.

Real resilience is ugly. It's the tree that grows around a nail instead of dying. It's the person who gets fired, then spends six months depressed, then starts a business from the floor of their bedroom because rent is due and no one else is coming to help.

Research on resilience—actual studies, not LinkedIn posts—shows that resilient people don't bounce back. They sink first. They feel the full weight of the disaster. They just don't stay at the bottom forever.

The word has been cleaned up for polite company. But resilience originally meant the capacity to suffer and continue. Not to skip the suffering. To go through it.

Obsession

What people think it means: Really liking something. Being very focused. A harmless passion.

What it actually means: A state where the thing you care about begins to crowd out everything else, including sleep, relationships, basic hygiene, and sometimes sanity.

The word shares roots with "besieged." An obsession doesn't visit. It lays siege to your mind. It cuts off supply lines to other interests. It demands attention at 3 a.m. when you should be sleeping. It makes you the kind of person who forgets to eat because you're chasing a problem.

This is not healthy. Most of the time, it's not even productive. But here's the uncomfortable truth: most things worth building require a period of obsession. Not forever. But for a stretch. The startup founder working eighty-hour weeks. The athlete training through injury. The writer who cancels plans to sit alone with a sentence.

Obsession is dangerous. That's why the word exists. We've softened it into "passion" when the original meaning was closer to "possession." Something takes you over. And sometimes, that's the only way the hard things get done.

Commitment

What people think it means: Showing up. Not quitting. Being reliable.

What it actually means: A decision made in advance that you will not reconsider, even when the reasons for making it disappear.

This is the brutal one. Commitment is not about how you feel on the good days. Anyone can stay committed when things are working. Commitment reveals itself when the original motivation is gone.

You committed to the marriage. Then your spouse changes. Then you change. The reasons you got married no longer exist. Commitment says: doesn't matter.

You committed to the business. Then the market shifts. Then a partner leaves. Then you realize you hate the work. Commitment says: figure it out anyway.

Research on commitment—in psychology, in organizational behavior—shows that the strongest commitments are the ones made publicly, with consequences for breaking them. Because the human mind is wired to rationalize quitting. Commitment is the lock you put on the door before the temptation arrives.

We use the word casually. "I'm committed to this diet." No you're not. Not if you break it on Tuesday because someone brought cake. Real commitment doesn't bend for cake.

Passion

What people think it means: Excitement. Energy. Loving what you do.

What it actually means: A willingness to suffer for something because the suffering feels meaningful.

The word comes from the Latin pati, meaning "to suffer." The Passion of Christ. The Passion of the martyrs. Passion originally meant the thing you endured, not the thing that made you happy.

Somewhere in the last century, we flipped it. Now passion means the opposite of suffering. It means joy, fulfillment, doing what you love. But that's not the original deal.

Real passion—the kind that produces work worth seeing—involves long hours of boredom, repeated failure, and the quiet humiliation of being bad at something for years before you get good. The passion isn't the reward at the end. The passion is the engine that makes the suffering tolerable.

If you're not willing to be bored, to fail, to look stupid, to do the same thing a thousand times until your hands ache—then you don't have passion. You have enthusiasm. And enthusiasm is cheap. It leaves when things get hard.

Patience

What people think it means: Waiting calmly. Not getting angry. Being peaceful.

What it actually means: The ability to tolerate uncertainty without abandoning the process.

This is the most misunderstood word on the list.

Patience is not about time. It's about uncertainty. You can wait a decade for something you're sure will come. That's not patience. That's just delayed gratification.

Patience is when you don't know if it will come. When the outcome is genuinely uncertain. When you've done everything right and still nothing happens and you have no guarantee that anything will ever happen—and you keep going anyway.

Research on delayed gratification—the famous marshmallow experiments—is often misread as patience training. But those children knew the reward was coming if they waited. That's not patience. That's a sure thing.

Real patience is the farmer planting seeds in drought, not knowing if the rains will come. It's the writer finishing a novel with no publisher. It's the entrepreneur launching a product with no customers.

Patience is not calm. It's often anxious, frustrated, and exhausted. It just doesn't quit.

Why This Matters

Here's why I'm writing this.

When we soften these words, we lose the map. Resilience becomes "staying positive" instead of "surviving being broken." Obsession becomes "focus" instead of "possession." Commitment becomes "reliability" instead of "pre-decision." Passion becomes "joy" instead of "willingness to suffer." Patience becomes "waiting" instead of "tolerating uncertainty."

And then we wonder why we feel like failures when the easy versions don't work.

You try to be resilient by smiling through pain. That doesn't work because real resilience requires feeling the pain.

You try to be passionate by finding work you love. That doesn't work because real passion is about enduring the work you've chosen.

You try to be patient by waiting calmly. That doesn't work because real patience is about continuing without calm.

The words were never meant to be comfortable. They were meant to describe the hard parts. We sanded off the edges and now we're surprised when they don't cut.

Here's a better way: take the words back. Use them honestly.

Resilience means you will break, and that's fine, because breaking isn't the end.

Obsession means something will take you over, and that's dangerous, but sometimes necessary.

Commitment means you will stay when you want to leave, because you decided before the wanting started.

Passion means you will suffer, and that suffering will be the measure of how much you actually care.

Patience means you will not know, and you will continue anyway.

These are not comfortable words. They were never supposed to be.

The Third Eye View on Honey

Most people think honey is a health food. The jars say "natural." The internet calls it a superfood. Your grandmother puts it in tea for a cold. And somewhere along the way, we decided that because it comes from bees instead of a factory, we can use it freely.

But here's the question that's been sitting in my head: is honey actually healthy, or is it just sugar with better marketing?

Let's cut through the hype.

What Honey Actually Is

Open any jar of honey and you're looking at something that's roughly eighty percent sugar. Mostly fructose and glucose, the same basic building blocks as table sugar. Another twenty percent is water. And then, in trace amounts, there are some antioxidants, enzymes, and tiny quantities of vitamins and minerals.

That's it. That's the miracle food.

The beneficial compounds are real. Honey does have mild anti-inflammatory properties. It does contain antioxidants that lab studies find interesting. But these exist in such small amounts that you'd need to eat dangerous quantities of sugar to get a meaningful dose.

This is the first thing the marketing doesn't tell you: honey is sugar with a few bonus nutrients attached. Not medicine. Not a free pass.

The Insulin Question

Before we talk about honey and insulin, let's be clear about what insulin actually is.

Insulin is a hormone that helps move sugar from your blood into your cells to be used for energy or stored. Think of it as a key. Sugar knocks on your cell doors. Insulin opens them.

Here's why this matters for honey.

When you consume honey—or any sugar—your blood sugar rises. Your body detects this and releases insulin. Insulin then helps store that sugar. Some of it gets used as energy right away. The rest gets stored as fat for later.

This is normal. This is how bodies work. The problem isn't insulin itself. The problem is too much insulin, too often.

More honey means more sugar entering your bloodstream. More sugar means more insulin release. More insulin release, over time, means your cells start ignoring the signal. They get tired of the key turning in the lock. That's insulin resistance.

And insulin resistance is the first step toward weight gain, metabolic syndrome, and eventually diabetes.

So when you add a generous amount of honey to your tea, you're not getting a free health boost. You're triggering the exact same hormonal response as someone drinking a soda. Your body doesn't read the label. It just counts the sugar.

Compared to white sugar, honey has a slightly lower glycemic index. This means it raises blood sugar a bit more slowly. The fructose in honey is processed by the liver rather than flooding your system all at once. That's a small advantage.

But lower isn't low. And small advantages disappear when you use large amounts.

The person who uses three tablespoons of honey a day "because it's healthy" is getting the same insulin response as someone drinking a can of Coke. The body doesn't care about intentions. It cares about biochemistry.

The Tea Problem

This is where the real disconnect lives.

Most people don't use a teaspoon of honey. They squeeze the bottle until the tea changes color. A typical serving of honey in a mug is one to three tablespoons.

One tablespoon of honey has about seventeen grams of sugar. Three tablespoons puts you at fifty-one grams.

For comparison, a can of Coca-Cola has thirty-nine grams.

That cup of "healthy" tea just exceeded the sugar content of a soda. And because it feels virtuous, many people have two or three cups a day.

This is the trap. The reputation makes you comfortable with quantities you would never tolerate from white sugar.

The Actual Benefits

Let's be fair. Honey does have advantages over refined sugar.

Raw honey contains small amounts of antioxidants called flavonoids and phenolic acids. These have been shown in studies to reduce oxidative stress. Some research suggests honey can modestly improve cholesterol profiles. There's decent evidence for its antimicrobial properties—which is why it's used on wounds in clinical settings.

For a cold, honey genuinely does soothe a sore throat better than cough syrup in some studies.

But here's what the studies also show: these benefits appear only at controlled, moderate doses. The moment you start using honey as a free-for-all, the sugar damage outweighs any antioxidant benefit.

You cannot out-supplement a bad diet. And you cannot out-antioxidant an insulin spike.

When Honey Becomes Unhealthy

Honey becomes problematic in three clear scenarios.

First, when you consume it in large amounts. This seems obvious, but people genuinely don't realize how much they're using. A drizzle here, a spoonful there, and suddenly you've added fifty grams of sugar to your day without eating anything sweet.

Second, when you use it as a free pass. The logic goes: "It's natural, so I don't have to count it." This is the most dangerous mindset. Your pancreas doesn't have a natural-versus-processed sensor.

Third, when you add it on top of an already high-sugar diet. If you're already eating bread, rice, fruit, and the occasional sweet, adding honey pushes you over the edge even if each individual source seems reasonable.

Studies on diabetics are particularly telling. Some research shows no benefit or even negative effects when diabetics increase honey intake. The body still struggles. The insulin still strains. The natural label doesn't help.

How Much Is Actually Okay

Let's get specific.

For a healthy person with no metabolic issues, up to one tablespoon of honey per day is generally fine. That's about seventeen grams of sugar. If you're replacing white sugar with honey, this is a net improvement—not because the honey is healing you, but because you've reduced your overall sugar load.

For someone watching blood sugar—prediabetic, diabetic, or insulin resistant—the number drops. Half a tablespoon, maybe less. And even then, only if you're not getting sugar from other sources.

The key is total sugar intake, not the source. The World Health Organization recommends keeping added sugar below ten percent of daily calories. For a two-thousand-calorie diet, that's about fifty grams of total added sugar. Honey counts toward that number. Every gram.

So if you have one tablespoon of honey, you've used about a third of your daily sugar budget. Two tablespoons, two-thirds. Three tablespoons, and you're over the limit before you've eaten anything else sweet.

The Third Eye View

Here's where I land after looking at the research.

Honey is healthier than white sugar. Slightly. At the margins. For specific uses like soothing a cough or replacing refined sugar in small amounts.

But honey is also overrated. Dramatically so. The gap between reputation and reality is enormous. People treat it like medicine when it's functionally a condiment.

The way most people actually use honey—pouring it freely into tea, drizzling it over yogurt, adding it to smoothies—cancels any possible benefit. At those quantities, honey is just sugar with a halo.

Insulin is the storage signal triggered by sugar. And honey triggers it too—if you use enough.

Does it spike insulin? Yes. At the amounts people commonly use, absolutely yes.

Is it healthy? In small amounts, as a replacement for refined sugar, yes. As a free-for-all, no.

The most honest thing I can say is this: honey is sugar. Good sugar, interesting sugar, sugar with a few extra molecules that do nice things. But still sugar. And sugar, no matter where it comes from, demands respect.

Your grandmother was right to put a teaspoon in tea for a cold. She was not right to drink three mugs a day.

The dose makes the poison. Always has. Even when the poison comes in a bear-shaped bottle.

The Science of Not Liking Things

Your brain is running a constant calculation, every second of every day: is this good for me or bad for me?

That's it. That's the engine behind every preference, every instinct, every moment of disgust or delight. We like to think our tastes are sophisticated, our dislikes are reasoned, our choices are conscious. But underneath all that, there's a much older system at work—one that evolved long before you had opinions about music or food or politics.

Let's walk through what actually happens when you decide you don't like something.

Why Disliking Kept Us Alive

Imagine your ancestor, ten thousand years ago, walking through the bush. She finds a piece of fruit, takes a bite, and it tastes bitter—immediately she spits it out. Hours later, she's fine. Her companion, who ate the whole thing, is now vomiting and delirious.

The one who disliked the bitter taste survived. The one who ignored the signal didn't.

That's evolution at work. Disliking isn't a flaw. It's a feature. A protection system.

Rotten food triggers disgust so you don't poison yourself. A sudden loud noise triggers fear so you jump back from whatever might be falling. Social rejection triggers actual physical pain—neuroscience has shown this—so you stay connected to your tribe, because alone in the wild, you die.

The people who didn't have these aversions didn't live long enough to have children. You exist because your ancestors disliked the right things.

Your Brain Has Dedicated "Dislike" Circuits

Here's something most people don't realize: disliking isn't just the absence of liking. It has its own machinery.

The amygdala, deep in your brain, is constantly scanning for threats. It doesn't wait for conscious thought. It reacts in milliseconds. That jolt you feel when something moves in your peripheral vision? That's the amygdala.

The insula handles disgust—not just from bad tastes, but from moral disgust too. When you hear about someone betraying a friend and you feel that visceral revulsion, that's your insula lighting up.

The nucleus accumbens, usually discussed as a pleasure center, also evaluates displeasure. It's not one-sided. It's a scale, and it's always weighing.

Even dopamine, the famous "pleasure chemical," isn't just about reward. It helps process aversive signals too. Your brain uses the same basic currency to tell you both what to approach and what to run from.

How Your Brain Tags Everything as Good or Bad

Every experience you have gets a valence tag. Positive means approach. Negative means avoid.

This happens fast. Faster than you realize. You walk into a room and feel something off before you can name it. You meet someone and get a bad feeling without knowing why. That's your valence system working below conscious awareness.

Scientists call this the circumplex model of affect—all emotions exist on a spectrum from pleasant to unpleasant, and your brain is constantly plotting where everything lands.

When you say "I don't like this," what you're really saying is: my brain has labeled this as negative value, and my body is already preparing to avoid it.

You Weren't Born Disliking Most Things

Some dislikes come built-in. Bitter tastes, for example. Newborns make the same disgust face whether you give them bitter water or sour milk. Nobody taught them that.

But most dislikes are learned.

Classical conditioning is the simplest form. You eat something and get sick afterward—even if the food wasn't the cause—your brain may permanently tag that food as bad. One bad experience can override years of neutral ones.

Reinforcement learning is more active. You try something, it hurts, you learn not to try it again. Your brain keeps a running ledger of outcomes, and negative ones get weighted heavily.

Social learning might be the most powerful. You don't have to get sick yourself. If you watch someone else recoil from something, your brain learns the aversion secondhand. This is why cultural dislikes spread. Nobody in your community eats a certain thing? You won't either. Not because you tried it and hated it. Because you learned it was hateable.

Disgust: The Strongest Form of Not Liking

Disgust is special. It's the most biologically rooted of all aversions.

Think about what triggers it: bad smells, rot, contamination, bodily fluids, things that look diseased. Your brain is basically screaming: pathogen risk, stay away.

The insula and limbic system work together to produce that full-body recoil. It's not intellectual. It's not reasoned. It's your nervous system taking over because somewhere in evolutionary history, people who weren't disgusted by these things died from what they touched.

What's fascinating is that disgust migrated from physical contamination to moral contamination. You can feel disgusted by a betrayal, by an injustice, by someone's behavior. Your brain is using the same circuitry to say: this person is dangerous to associate with, avoid them.

Why People Dislike Different Things

If disliking is so hardwired, why don't we all hate the same things?

Three reasons.

Genetics: Some people have more sensitive taste receptors. Some have more reactive amygdala. Some have higher baseline threat detection. You don't choose your nervous system's sensitivity.

Personality: People who score high on neuroticism experience more negative emotions more intensely. Their "dislike" dial is turned up across the board. People who score high on openness are more willing to override initial aversions in favor of novelty.

Culture: What's disgusting in one place is dinner in another. Your brain's aversion circuitry is shaped by what your community teaches you to avoid. This isn't weakness. It's efficiency. Why learn everything from scratch when you can inherit ten thousand years of local wisdom about what's dangerous?

The Big Picture

When you dislike something, here's what actually happened:

Your brain detected a stimulus—a taste, a sound, a person, an idea.

It ran that stimulus through your valence system, probably before you were consciously aware of it.

It activated emotional circuits—fear, disgust, anger, or simple negative affect.

It flagged the outcome in your learning systems, so next time you encounter the same thing, you'll avoid it faster.

And then it generated the conscious feeling you call "not liking this," along with a story about why.

The story is often wrong. Your brain is terrible at explaining its own calculations. But the calculation itself is ancient, efficient, and designed for one purpose: keeping you alive long enough to reproduce.

That's what disliking is. Not a flaw in your character. Not a failure of open-mindedness. A survival system that's been working for millions of years.

The question isn't whether you should dislike things. You will. Your brain won't let you stop.

The question is whether you occasionally override the system when the system is wrong—when the aversion is learned but not useful, when the disgust is cultural but not harmful, when the fear is ancient but not relevant.

Because that's the other thing your brain can do. It can learn new valences. It can unlearn old ones.

It just takes work.

The Men History Doesn't Forget

Seven men. Seven lessons. A single thread running through all of them: history was never shaped by nice thinkers.

Not cruel men, necessarily. Not monsters. But men who saw reality clearly and moved accordingly while others hesitated, rationalized, or hoped for the best.

Let's walk through what they actually understood.

Niccolò Machiavelli: Fear Commands What Love Cannot

Machiavelli wrote The Prince in 1513, after being tortured, exiled, and pushed out of Florentine politics by the Medici family. He had every reason to write a bitter book. Instead, he wrote a clear-eyed one, drawn from decades of watching how power actually moved.

His core observation: people change their feelings fast, but they rarely lose their respect for power. Kindness is admired in the moment. Strength is obeyed long after.

This wasn't cynicism. It was observation. A ruler loved by his people faces disaster the moment a stronger power appears, because love is soft. A ruler respected—perhaps even feared—faces the same disaster with his army still behind him, his officials still loyal, his system still intact.

If you have to choose, Machiavelli argued, choose to be respected. Influence built on emotion dies the moment feelings shift. Influence built on structural reality survives.

The lesson isn't "be cruel." It's "stop pretending that being liked is the same as being relied upon."

Alexander the Great: Boldness Rewrites Destiny

Alexander did not wait for the perfect moment. He created it.

By the time he died at thirty-two, he had conquered the Persian Empire, Egypt, and parts of India. Not because he was reckless—he studied terrain, logistics, enemy psychology—but because he understood that waiting for certainty is waiting for death.

At the Battle of Issus, his army was outnumbered more than two to one. Darius of Persia had chosen the ground, arranged his forces, prepared every advantage. Alexander's generals urged caution. Alexander charged.

He won not because the odds were in his favor. He won because he refused to let the odds define what was possible.

The lesson: history does not remember the careful. It remembers the ones willing to risk everything on a conviction. Not recklessness—conviction. There's a difference. Recklessness ignores the odds. Conviction calculates them and moves anyway.

Julius Caesar: Betrayal Sits at Your Table

The most painful lessons are written in blood.

On the Ides of March, 44 BC, Julius Caesar walked into the Roman Senate. Sixty senators surrounded him with knives. One of them, Brutus, was his protégé. Another, Decimus, was his friend. They called themselves liberators. They called themselves loyal until the moment they weren't.

Caesar had pardoned most of them after defeating them in civil war. He had trusted them. He had believed that generosity would be repaid with loyalty.

Instead, he bled out on the Senate floor, his blood soaking the base of Pompey's statue—Pompey, his greatest enemy, who he had also pardoned and who was already dead.

The lesson: real danger does not stand outside the gates. It sits close, smiles often, and calls itself loyal. Power requires trust with limits. Not paranoia—just accounting for human nature. People change. Ambitions shift. The person who would never betray you today might become someone else tomorrow.

Respect them. Love them, even. But never hand anyone the power to destroy you.

Napoleon Bonaparte: Small Edges Win Big Wars

Napoleon dominated Europe for two decades not through miraculous victories but through accumulated advantages.

He studied terrain before battles. He timed attacks for when the sun would be in enemy eyes. He organized his armies into independent corps that could move faster than opposing forces. He calculated supply lines, weather patterns, morale.

Austerlitz, he let the allies think they had the advantage, then struck exactly when overconfidence made them careless. At Jena, he moved so fast the Prussian army never had time to form properly.

None of these were grand, decisive advantages. Each was a small edge. Stacked together, they became unstoppable.

The lesson: power is rarely taken in one grand move. It's stolen quietly, piece by piece, while everyone else is distracted. Most people want the dramatic victory, the moment of glory. Napoleon understood that glory is just what you call it after you've won the tenth small battle in a row.

Genghis Khan: Control the System, Not the People

Genghis Khan built the largest contiguous empire in human history. He did it not through brute force alone, but through architecture.

Before Genghis, steppe tribes fought each other constantly. He united them under a single legal code, the Yassa, which applied equally to everyone. He created relay stations across Asia so messages could travel hundreds of miles in days. He employed spies, built intelligence networks, controlled information flow.

He understood that conquering people was temporary. Conquering the system they lived inside was permanent.

His armies were terrifying, yes. But what made resistance feel pointless was the structure. By the time you considered fighting, you were already inside a machine designed to make fighting you lose.

The lesson: real domination isn't about strength. It's about owning the architecture everyone else is forced to live inside. The man who controls the pipeline, the platform, the rules—he doesn't need to fight every battle. He's already won.

John D. Rockefeller: Own the Pipeline

Rockefeller built Standard Oil into a monopoly not by selling more oil than anyone else, but by controlling how oil moved.

While competitors fought over customers, Rockefeller quietly bought refineries. Then pipelines. Then transportation. By the time anyone realized what was happening, they couldn't operate without him. If you wanted to get oil from Pennsylvania to New York, you paid Rockefeller. There was no other way.

He understood a brutal truth: the man who controls the supply controls the entire game. Fighting for customers is exhausting. Fighting for the infrastructure everyone depends on—that's permanent.

The lesson applies far beyond oil. In any industry, any ecosystem, the question is: what's the bottleneck? What do people need that only you can provide? That's where the real power lives. Stop fighting for small wins at the surface. Take the position that makes everyone else dependent on you.

Winston Churchill: Break or Become the Anchor

Hitler had conquered Poland, France, Belgium, the Netherlands. The British army had just been evacuated from Dunkirk in chaos, leaving most of its equipment behind. Invasion seemed imminent.

And Churchill stood in front of Parliament and said:

"We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields and in the streets, we shall fight in the hills; we shall never surrender."

Not optimism. Not comfort. Backbone.

He didn't pretend the situation was good. He didn't offer false hope. He offered refusal—refusal to bend, refusal to break, refusal to even consider the possibility of defeat.

That refusal became contagious. A nation staring into the abyss found someone willing to stand at the edge and stare back.

The lesson: when the world is collapsing, people don't need comfort. They need an anchor. They need someone whose knees don't shake. Leadership isn't smiling through chaos. It's staying calm while everyone else trembles, becoming the fixed point they can hold onto.

The man who controls himself in a crisis becomes the one everyone else is forced to follow.

Napoleon Hill: Empires Begin in the Mind

Napoleon Hill spent twenty years studying successful people—Andrew Carnegie, Henry Ford, Thomas Edison—trying to understand what separated them from everyone else.

His conclusion: before power exists outside, it exists as a fixed idea inside.

The men who built empires didn't wait for permission. They didn't hope circumstances would change. They saw what they wanted, decided it was possible, and refused to let doubt erode that decision. Weak thoughts build cages. Strong beliefs build kingdoms.

This isn't magical thinking. It's not "visualize and it will appear." It's deeper than that. It's about what happens in the space between stimulus and response. The man whose inner world is undisciplined reacts to every setback, every criticism, every difficulty. The man whose inner world is trained holds steady, adapts, continues.

The lesson: if your inner world is undisciplined, your outer world will always be limited. A dangerous mind—capable, steady, clear—is the first asset of a powerful man.

What They Understood

Seven men. Seven eras. Seven different kinds of power.

But the thread is visible: they all saw reality as it was, not as they wished it to be. They didn't pretend people were more loyal than they are. They didn't wait for conditions to become perfect. They didn't confuse niceness with strength.

They built systems that lasted, made decisions that mattered, and left marks that centuries haven't erased.

The question their lives leave us with isn't "would you want to be them?" It's "what are you building, and will it survive when feelings shift, when pressure comes, when the easy path disappears?"

Because history remembers builders. It remembers people who saw clearly and acted anyway.

The rest are just names in footnotes.

The Late Adopter's Advantage: Why Tanzania's Technological "Delay" Might Save Us

Let me ask you something.

Have you seen the videos coming out of San Francisco? The ones where a car drives itself—empty seats, no hands on the wheel, just a steering wheel turning on its own like a ghost is driving?

I watched one last week. A woman got in, tapped her phone, and the car pulled away while she scrolled Instagram. No driver. No conversation. No human at all.

Then I walked to Kariakoo later that day and watched three men push a truck that had stalled on the bridge. They weren't angry about it. They just pushed, sweat soaking through their shirts, and when the engine caught, they laughed and slapped palms like they'd won something.

Two worlds. Same planet.

The question people keep asking is: Why is Tanzania slow with technology?

Maybe the better question is: What are we being slow for?

The Numbers Nobody Talks About

In America, the job displacement conversation is already old.

Self-driving trucks are on highways in Arizona. Not tests—real trucks moving real freight with no one in the cab. The American Trucking Association says there are about 3.5 million truck drivers in the country. That's 3.5 million families whose primary income suddenly depends on whether the technology works better than the human.

In tech, the numbers are harder to track but easier to feel. Startups that once hired twenty junior developers now hire five senior ones plus AI tools. Marketing agencies that employed teams of writers now have one person managing AI content generation. Customer service centers that employed hundreds now run chatbots that handle 80% of queries before a human ever sees them.

The pattern is consistent: the technology arrives, the jobs leave, and the people are told to "retrain" without anyone explaining what they're supposed to retrain for.

What Tanzania Actually Has

Walk through Kariakoo market on a Saturday. What do you see?

People. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Selling, buying, negotiating, carrying, shouting, laughing. Every transaction is a human interaction. Every price is negotiated face to face. Every relationship is built over time, not through an app.

This is not inefficiency. This is employment at scale.

The informal economy in Tanzania employs something like 80% of the workforce. That's not a statistic to be embarrassed about—it's a reality to be understood. Those jobs exist because there is no automated alternative. You cannot replace a mama ntillie with a vending machine because the value she provides isn't just the food. It's the conversation, the credit she extends when she knows you're good for it, the way she remembers that you don't like too much salt.

Technology cannot do that. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

The Late Adopter's Advantage

Here's something the early adopters don't tell you: being first means being the crash test dummy.

The United States and China are racing to implement AI, automation, self-driving systems. In the process, they're discovering all the ways these technologies break. The ethical problems. The unemployment crises. The social friction that happens when you replace human connection with efficiency.

Tanzania gets to watch.

We get to see what works and what destroys. We get to observe which technologies actually improve life and which ones just enrich shareholders. We get to adopt not the first version, but the version that's been tested, fixed, and made useful.

There's a term for this: leapfrogging. It's what happened with mobile money. While Western countries were still figuring out credit card terminals, Tanzania jumped straight to M-Pesa. No legacy infrastructure to maintain. No old systems to replace. Just the good version, implemented when it was ready.

The same could happen with AI, with automation, with everything else that's coming.

But The Question Still Stands

What happens when it arrives?

Because it will arrive. The technology is not going away. The question isn't if self-driving trucks reach the Dar-Morogoro highway. It's when. The question isn't if AI becomes capable of handling customer service in Kiswahili. It's how soon.

And when that happens, the 80% of Tanzanians working in informal, human-to-human transactions will face the same pressure American truck drivers are facing now.

The difference is that we have time. We have the advantage of watching the crash from a distance.

What We Could Do With That Time

Here's where the conversation gets serious.

If we know automation is coming—if we can see clearly that certain jobs will be replaced—then the question becomes: what do we build in the meantime?

Not panic. Not Luddite fantasies about smashing machines. But actual, practical preparation.

Some directions:

Emphasize what machines cannot do. Relationship building. Trust. Cultural nuance. Creativity that isn't just remixing existing work. The things that require a human body and a human history.

Build infrastructure for adaptation. Not just technical training, but the kind of flexible thinking that lets people pivot when industries shift. This is harder than it sounds, because our education system is built on memorization, not adaptation.

Watch carefully. Not just the technology, but the social effects. Which jobs disappear first elsewhere? Which new jobs appear to replace them? What skills do those new jobs require?

Move at our own pace. The pressure to "catch up" is often manufactured by people selling technology. Catching up to what? To unemployment? To social disruption? To systems designed without our interests in mind?

A Different Kind of Development

Maybe Tanzania's "slowness" isn't failure. Maybe it's resistance by default—a society structured in a way that cannot be easily automated because its value is in the human connections, not the transactions.

That won't last forever. The machines are coming. But they're coming on a road we can still choose to build carefully.

The question isn't whether technology will arrive.

The question is whether we'll have built something worth keeping when it does.

The Personal MBA: Why You Don't Need Business School to Understand Business

Let me tell you a secret that business schools won't advertise: you can learn everything they teach in an MBA program for the price of a few books and a month of focused reading.

The catch? You have to actually want to learn.

Josh Kaufman's The Personal MBA: A World-Class Business Education in One Volume is built on this premise. Kaufman spent years distilling the core concepts of business—the ones taught in elite programs like Harvard and Stanford—into a single, readable book. No case study padding. No networking events. No six-figure tuition. Just the ideas, explained clearly.

Here's what it covers and why it matters to normal people trying to build something.

What Actually Is Business?

Kaufman starts with a definition so simple it feels obvious, which is usually a sign you're dealing with something true:

"A business is a repeatable process that creates and delivers something of value that other people want or need, at a price they're willing to pay, in a way that meets their expectations and provides enough profit to make it worthwhile."

Break that down and you get the five parts of every business:

Value creation: You make something people want. A product, a service, a solution to a problem.

Marketing: You find people who want it and let them know you exist.

Sales: You convince them to exchange money for what you've made.

Value delivery: You actually give them the thing, and it works the way you promised.

Finance: You manage the money so you don't die.

Every business, from a street food stall to a multinational corporation, is just these five things working together. If one breaks, the whole thing wobbles.

The Core Mental Models

What makes Kaufman's approach different is his focus on mental models—simple frameworks for thinking about complex problems. A few that stick with you:

The Iron Law of the Market: If you don't have a market, nothing else matters. You can build the most elegant product in the world, but if nobody wants to buy it, you don't have a business. You have an expensive hobby.

12x the Value: People don't buy products. They buy solutions to problems. Price isn't about what something costs to make. It's about the value it provides. If your product solves a problem worth solving, charge accordingly.

The 80/20 Principle: Also called Pareto's Law. Roughly 80% of your results come from 20% of your efforts. Figure out which 20% is doing the work. Do more of that. Stop doing the rest.

Five-Fold Why: When something goes wrong, ask why five times. The first answer is usually symptoms. The fifth is the actual cause.

How People Actually Decide to Buy

One of the most useful sections is on what Kaufman calls the Five Forces of Customer Action. Before anyone buys anything, they go through a sequence:

  1. They have a problem they notice. Something feels wrong, missing, or painful.
  2. They consider solutions. What could fix this? They might buy, or they might do nothing, or they might jury-rig a temporary fix.
  3. They evaluate options. Your product versus alternatives versus doing nothing.
  4. They overcome friction. Price, effort, risk, anything standing between them and buying.
  5. They actually purchase and use. Then they decide whether it worked.

Understanding this sequence helps you see why people don't buy even when your product is objectively good. Maybe they don't realize they have the problem. Maybe your solution seems too hard. Maybe the price feels wrong even if the math works.

Most business failures aren't about the product. They're about misunderstanding one of these steps.

Why This Book Matters for Normal People

Here's the thing about business school: it's designed for people who want to work at large corporations or investment banks. It's about managing existing systems, not building new ones. It costs hundreds of thousands of dollars and two years of your life.

The Personal MBA is designed for people who want to:

  • Start something of their own.
  • Understand how the business they work for actually functions.
  • Make better decisions without relying on expensive consultants.
  • Avoid getting talked into bad deals by people who use fancy jargon.

The book won't give you a credential. It won't get you into networking events with private equity partners. What it will give you is a working mental model of how value gets created, delivered, and exchanged in the world.

For most people, that's worth more than the degree anyway.

What Kaufman Leaves Out

To be fair: the book won't teach you accounting in depth. It won't give you Excel models. It won't connect you to alumni networks or corporate recruiters.

What it does is give you the conceptual foundation. The accounting can be learned from YouTube. The Excel from practice. The network you build by actually doing work that matters.

Kaufman's argument—and I think he's right—is that the core ideas are the hardest part. Once you understand how businesses actually work, you can fill in the technical details as you go.

A Final Thought

I meet a lot of people who want to start something but feel paralyzed by what they don't know. They think business is a secret language spoken only by people in suits with framed degrees on their walls.

It's not.

Business is just figuring out what people need and finding a way to give it to them sustainably. That's it. Everything else—the jargon, the case studies, the three-letter acronyms—is decoration.

The Personal MBA strips away the decoration. What's left is surprisingly simple. Not easy, but simple.

And sometimes simple is enough to start.

Surrealism: The Art of Unlearning Reality

SALVADOR DALÍ
"Surrealism is destructive, but it destroys only what it considers to be shackles limiting our vision."
— Salvador Dalí (1904–1989)

What It Is

Surrealism began in Paris in 1924, but its roots go deeper—into the trenches of World War I, into the consulting rooms of Sigmund Freud, into a growing suspicion that the world as presented to us was a lie.

The movement's founder, André Breton, a poet and former medical student who had worked with shell-shocked soldiers, defined it this way in his first manifesto:

"Pure psychic automatism... the dictation of thought, in the absence of any control exercised by reason, exempt from any aesthetic or moral concern."

In simpler words: unfiltered mind. Thoughts before they get dressed up for company. Dreams before they're edited into sense.

The surrealists believed that beneath the polite surface of everyday life—beneath manners, logic, and the 9-to-5—lay a deeper reality. A truer reality. One where contradictions could coexist, where a clock could melt, where a man could have a drawer growing from his chest, where the irrational made its own kind of perfect sense.

They were not trying to escape reality. They were trying to expand it.

The Man With The Melting Clocks

Salvador Dalí was born in Figueres, Catalonia, in 1904, five years before his older brother—also named Salvador—died. His parents told him he was the reincarnation of the dead brother. This, as you might imagine, did things to a person.

Dalí was technically brilliant. He could paint like the old masters—realistic, precise, every detail rendered with photographic care. But he used that skill to paint things that could never exist.

His most famous work, The Persistence of Memory (1931), shows three soft watches melting in a barren landscape. One hangs over a dead tree branch. Another drapes itself over a mysterious fleshy creature. A third drips over the edge of a platform.

The story goes that Dalí got the idea watching a Camembert cheese melt in the sun.

But the painting isn't about cheese. It's about the failure of time to contain experience. It's about memory being softer than we admit. It's about a world where the rules don't hold.

Dalí called his method the "paranoiac-critical method"—a way of seeing the world so intensely that reality itself began to warp. He claimed to remember being in his mother's womb. He walked around with a piece of cuttlefish balanced on his head because he liked the texture. He delivered lectures wearing a deep-sea diving suit, nearly suffocating before someone removed the helmet.

Was he genius or madman?

The surrealists would say the question itself is a shackle.

What They Were Destroying

Breton and his circle—artists like Max Ernst, René Magritte, Joan Miró—believed that Western civilization had become a prison. Rationality, they argued, had been used to justify the slaughter of millions in the war. Progress had delivered industrialised death. Logic had become a weapon.

They wanted to destroy:

  • The tyranny of reason — The idea that everything must make linear sense.
  • Bourgeois respectability — The performance of normalcy that hid deeper hungers.
  • The separation of art from life — The notion that creativity belonged in museums, not in how you lived.
  • The dictatorship of the conscious mind — The belief that we should only trust what we can control.

What they wanted to build was harder to name. But you can see it in their work:

  • Max Ernst's forests, where nature becomes menacing and alive.
  • Magritte's pipe that announces, "This is not a pipe," reminding us that representations are not the thing itself.
  • Miró's biomorphic shapes, floating and dancing like creatures from a private mythology.

They wanted to put the dream world on equal footing with the waking one. To suggest that what we call "reality" is just one layer—and perhaps not the most interesting one.

Surrealism in Real Life

You don't have to paint melting clocks to live surrealistically. The movement left behind tools, not just images.

In writing: Automatism—writing whatever comes, without editing, without stopping, letting the subconscious speak. William S. Burroughs would later develop the cut-up technique, slicing texts and rearranging them randomly, finding meaning in accident.

In daily life: The surrealists loved wandering cities aimlessly, a practice they called dérive—letting the streets pull you, noticing what you notice when you have no destination. They'd walk all night through Paris, following impulse, discovering corners of the city that maps ignored.

In how we think: Surrealism asks us to distrust the obvious. To question the first explanation. To entertain the possibility that a dream might be as true as a spreadsheet.

In popular culture: David Lynch's films, where a rabbit sits in a living room and nothing happens for minutes. Michel Gondry's music videos, where logic bends but emotion remains. The very concept of "vibes"—moods that can't be explained but are deeply felt—is a surrealist inheritance.

What It Means Now

We live in an age of information saturation. Everything is explained, categorized, algorithmically sorted. Surrealism feels more necessary than ever—not as an art movement, but as a posture.

It reminds us that not everything that's real can be measured. That a dream about your grandmother might matter more than your to-do list. That sometimes the most rational response to an irrational world is to stop being so damned rational.

Dalí said surrealism destroys shackles limiting our vision. The shackles change with every generation. Today they might be:

  • The endless scroll.
  • The pressure to optimize.
  • The belief that productivity is the highest good.
  • The fear of looking strange.

Surrealism, as an attitude, simply says: Look anyway. Imagine anyway. Let your mind wander where it wants.

You might not produce a masterpiece. But you might find yourself looking at a soft clock and seeing, for the first time, that time has never been as hard as they told you.

And that might be enough.

The Education of Emmanuel: A Hip-Hop Story

Emmanuel—"Geek" to his friends—didn't grow up with hip-hop. For years, music existed somewhere in the background of life, but it wasn't his thing. Not yet.

That changed recently. A few years ago, a friend, a hip hop enthusiast told Emmanuel, "Listen to this." Emmanuel nodded, tuned in, and pressed play. The song was by Dizasta Vina. And something clicked.

"Started with Dizasta Vina," he'd explain later. "Loved his songs cause of the contents."

It wasn't just the beat—it was the words. But Emmanuel couldn't catch every word by ear alone. So he did something that would come to define his entire relationship with hip-hop: he watched the lyrics.

On his phone, with the music video playing and subtitles on, he followed every line. If something moved too fast, he paused. Rewound. Watched again.

This wasn't passive listening. This was study.

The Immersion

Emmanuel developed a ritual. On Spotify, he'd pull up a song and read the lyrics in real-time as they scrolled. When the platform didn't have lyrics built in, he'd open Genius in another tab and read along there. YouTube became his classroom—music videos with subtitles, paused at every verse he wanted to sit with.

"If there are no lyrics on Spotify," he said, "I read them on Genius to understand the song. Yeah, they can get confusing sometimes."

His friends thought he was doing homework. In a way, he was.

After Dizasta came Kendrick Lamar. Then the floodgates opened. Eminem. J. Cole. NF. Joyner Lucas. Artists who treated words like tools—who built whole worlds inside sixteen bars. Emmanuel absorbed all of it, line by line, with his eyes as much as his ears.

He noticed things other listeners missed. The way a rhyme scheme could shift to signal emotional intensity. How a well-placed metaphor could reframe an entire verse. The difference between saying something and saying something.

The Understanding

One night, a friend asked him: "So did hip-hop add something or made you someone?"

Emmanuel thought about it. The question deserved an honest answer.

"It did to my mind," he wrote. "Changed my mindset. My perspectives about life, success, failure, relationships, and how to deal with... people."

He paused, then added: "I see it as informal education. Street education. It helped me see reality, man."

The lessons came through in fragments. A Kendrick bar about surviving pressure. An Eminem verse about grinding through criticism. A J. Cole line about staying true when everyone wants you to be something else.

Even the funny songs—like Lil Wayne's "Mrs. Officer," which made him laugh out loud—taught him that hip-hop didn't always have to be heavy. Sometimes it could just be human.

Emmanuel even tried writing his own bars once. "I got inspired to write some rap," he admitted, laughing, "but I can't write, man!"

Still, the impulse mattered. The desire to say something—that came from hip-hop.

The Present

Today, Emmanuel runs his own business. He calls it Raven.

Raven is a digital services company—web design, social media management, technical consulting. Emmanuel, the computer geek, building something from nothing. He's quiet by nature, but sharp. Observant. The kind of person who notices details other people miss.

People wonder how a guy in his twenties learned to think so strategically—to understand human nature, to navigate complexity, to see the story beneath the surface. The answer lives in his playlist.

Every negotiation reminds him of a lyric about reading between lines. Every challenge echoes a bar about perseverance. Every success carries the rhythm of artists who turned their experiences into something real.

The Lesson

"Last thing," Emmanuel said that night, closing the conversation. "It's inspiring and entertaining."

Simple words. But behind them was years of listening—really listening—with his eyes on the screen and the words in front of him.

The friend on the other end said: "Damn, that's really good, bro. The power of music."

Emmanuel didn't reply. He didn't need to.

He was probably already back to work—building Raven, one project at a time. Like a rapper in the studio. Like someone who learned that words, when you actually see them, can change everything.

The Unclaimed Salary: Why Tanzania Isn't Learning Chinese

In Kibaha's industrial zone, a factory manager's WhatsApp message pulses with quiet frustration: "Bado hajapatikana mtu." The position? Chinese translator and trainer. The salary? A net 1.3–1.5 million Tanzanian shillings monthly. The requirement? Functional Mandarin and Microsoft Office.

A year ago, a similar notice hung in Dar es salaam. The deadline passed. The position, I'm told, was filled only after months of searching.

On one side: a growing wave of Chinese industrial investment creating steady demand for linguistic and cultural bridges. On the other: a workforce educated in a system that hasn't yet pivoted to meet this new economic reality.

The Mismatch Between Perception and Reality

The core misunderstanding lies in what the job actually requires. These postings are not seeking scholars of classical Chinese literature. They need functional facilitators.

  • Technical Translation: Converting factory-floor terminology between Kiswahili, English, and Mandarin.
  • Operational Training: Explaining processes, safety protocols, and machine instructions.
  • Administrative Bridgework: Using Excel and Word to document procedures and smooth communication.

This is vocational, not academic. It's closer to a specialized technical skill than a humanities degree. Yet, the prospect of learning Mandarin is instantly categorized as "immensely difficult, lifelong pursuit", overshadowing the more attainable goal of industry-specific language acquisition.

The Allure of the Known Path

A three‑year degree — in Business, Public Administration, Sociology — carries recognized social weight. It feels like a key that fits many locks, even if those locks open to crowded fields with salaries half of what the factory offers. Learning Chinese for a factory job feels like forging a single, specific key. The perceived risk is higher: What if I invest time and the industry changes?

An Unseen Educational Gap

Where does one go to learn "Industrial Chinese for Tanzanian Contexts"? Universities offer broad academic tracks; private classes are generic. There is a missing layer of practical, accelerated, industry-focused training that could produce a job‑ready candidate in 12‑18 months.

The Real Cost of the Status Quo

  1. Operational inefficiency due to communication barriers.
  2. Missed managerial roles for Tanzanians who could oversee Sino-Tanzanian operations.
  3. Stagnant wages for those who default to traditional tracks with lower pay.

The Chinese industrial presence isn't a passing trend. It's a segment building infrastructure, establishing factories, and demanding a specific hybrid professional: the practical linguist‑operations specialist.

Conclusion: A Pragmatic Opportunity

This isn't a call for everyone to learn Chinese. It's a case study in economic signal reading. The market clearly signals demand for a skill set, backed by strong compensation. The barrier isn't just the language difficulty; it's the lack of a visible, efficient pipeline. For educational entrepreneurs, the signal is an opportunity. For young professionals, it's a prompt to look toward specific, high-demand competencies.

The factory in Kibaha will find its translator eventually. But the repeated urgent postings suggest a simple truth: the workforce that adapts first to the economy that exists will find its rewards waiting.

The Silent Engine: On Taxes and What Binds Nations

Every morning, as the sun rises over Dar es Salaam, a silent transaction occurs. It is not in the tea stalls nor the markets, but in the digital ledgers of the Tanzania Revenue Authority. A percentage of a shop's daily sales, a fraction of a salary, a slice of corporate profit—all flowing into a collective reservoir most will never see. This is not a modern invention. The pharaohs collected grain. Medieval kings demanded a tithe. The history of civilization is, in part, a history of this mandatory contribution: the tax.

The TRA, like all modern revenue authorities, is more than a collector; it is the circulatory system of the state. It translates economic activity into public capability. That tarmac road, that public school teacher's salary, the satellite monitoring the coastline—these are not spontaneous gifts. They are converted taxes. The authority's importance is not merely functional but existential; it is the mechanism that transforms a geographic territory with people in it into a nation with public goods.

Could a country survive without this body? One could imagine a society funded entirely by natural resource windfalls—a petro-state living off geological luck. But this replaces the tax collector with the commodity broker and ties national survival to global price swings. The purely charitable or fee-based model: you pay for the services you use, like a national subscription. Yet, who builds the road to the isolated village that cannot afford the 'subscription'? The concept of public good collapses into a marketplace of private affordability.

The junction, then, is not about the necessity of the mechanism—some form of collective funding is inevitable for collective life. The junction is about the conversion rate. It is about what happens in the space between the payment and the pavement. Is it efficient? Is it just? The taxpayer, from street vendor to CEO, is a stakeholder. The true test of any revenue authority is not in the amounts it collects, but in the legitimacy it earns.

The Simple Secrets of History's Greatest Leaders

We imagine great leaders as monumental figures, set apart by charisma or destiny. Yet, the truth is simpler. Leaders like Winston Churchill, Nelson Mandela, and Queen Elizabeth I wielded profound influence not through superhuman traits, but by mastering fundamental human principles—principles perfectly outlined in Dale Carnegie's How to Win Friends and Influence People.

Churchill, during Britain's darkest hours, didn't just give orders. He remembered names, acknowledged personal sacrifices, and framed the war effort as a shared, honorable struggle. He made every citizen feel like a crucial part of the story. This is Carnegie's principle of giving honest and sincere appreciation and making the other person feel important—on a national scale.

Nelson Mandela disarmed his jailers and later a divided nation by practicing seeing things from the other person's point of view. After 27 years in prison, he sought to understand the fears of white South Africans, addressing them not with blame but with respect. He used "yes, and" instead of "but," building bridges from common ground.

Even Queen Elizabeth I, during the Spanish Armada crisis, used what Carnegie calls "arousing an eager want." She famously aligned her personal safety with her troops: "I myself will take up arms… I will be your general." She translated a royal need into a common, passionate desire.

The pattern is clear. Great leadership is built with humble bricks: remembering a name, offering sincere praise, listening first. These leaders were masters of a simple truth: people will follow those who make them feel heard, respected, and part of something meaningful.

Their genius was applying the basics of human influence with immense consistency. They proved that to move millions, you start by authentically connecting with one.

— Guy Fei
